Alan Parkinson informed the Commission that the Association of Idaho Cities had

Commission. Alan then invited questions from the Commission regarding the
introductory training video.

years, the City also evaluates it at the five-year mark to determine whether any
adjustments are needed. Alan stated that a major rewrite is typically completed after ten
years to address significant changes that have occurred within the community and to
ensure the plan continues to reflect the City's long-term vision.

2. Urban Renewal – Proposed West Highway Urban Renewal Plan – Scott Miller (Action)

Presentation:

City Staff Scott Miller introduced himself as the administrator for the Rexburg Urban
Renewal Agency and explained that the Agency was pursuing the creation of a new
urban renewal district. He introduced Brad Kramer, a consultant with Perspective
Planning and Consulting, and turned the time over to him for the presentation.
Brad Kramer introduced himself and thanked the Commission members for their service
to the community. He explained that he had been hired by the Rexburg Urban Renewal
Agency to assist with the creation of the proposed West Highway Urban Renewal
District, located west of Highway 20 and north and south of Highway 33. Brad provided
an overview of the urban renewal process, explaining that the district had already
undergone several statutory review steps, including the preparation of an eligibility report
that had been approved by the Urban Renewal Agency, Madison County, and the
Rexburg City Council. He stated that the Commission's role was to review the proposed
plan and determine whether it was consistent with the City's Comprehensive Plan before
forwarding a recommendation to the City Council for final consideration.
Brad Kramer discussed the purpose of the proposed district and explained that tax
increment financing would be used to help fund infrastructure improvements and
economic development projects within the designated area. He noted that the district
had been determined to be economically feasible due to the significant growth and
development anticipated in the area. Brad reviewed several qualifying conditions
supporting the district's creation, including inadequate roadway infrastructure, costly
transportation improvements, lack of pedestrian facilities, outdated street patterns,
regional utility needs, lot layout challenges, and the complexity of coordinating
improvements among numerous property owners.
Brad Kramer further explained that the Urban Renewal District would not alter the City's
Comprehensive Plan or existing zoning designations. He reviewed how the proposed
district supported several goals identified in the Comprehensive Plan, including

infrastructure improvements, utility expansion, economic development, housing
opportunities, and business growth. He stated that tax increment financing could help
offset extraordinary development costs and provide incentives for investment in areas
where infrastructure expenses might otherwise discourage development.
Dan Hanna commented that it would be helpful to further explain the tax increment
financing process. Brad Kramer responded by describing how tax increment financing
works. He explained that when an urban renewal district is established, the current
taxable value of properties within the district becomes the base value, with taxes from
that base continuing to be distributed to the existing taxing entities. As development
occurs and property values increase, the additional tax revenue generated above the
base value is directed to the Urban Renewal Agency and reinvested into eligible projects
within the district boundaries. He noted that these funds can be used for improvements
such as roads, utilities, water and sewer infrastructure, parks, and other qualifying
projects. Alan Parkinson asked Brad to further clarify how property taxes are affected
by the district. Brad emphasized that urban renewal does not create a new tax or
increase existing taxes, but instead redirects a portion of future tax revenue growth to
fund improvements within the district. He also explained that school districts continue to
receive their tax revenues and that certain special districts may be exempt from
participation unless they choose to opt in.
Dan Hanna remarked that tax increment financing serves as a significant economic
development tool because it helps fund necessary infrastructure improvements, making
development projects more feasible and encouraging future business investment and
growth within the community.
Scott Miller reminded those speaking to use the microphone so their comments could
be accurately recorded. He then displayed a map of Rexburg's existing urban renewal
districts and provided an overview of each district's history and accomplishments.
Scott Miller explained that Rexburg currently has four urban renewal districts, with the
proposed West Highway District becoming the fifth. He reviewed the downtown district
and discussed how urban renewal funding has been used to support infrastructure
improvements and redevelopment projects in that area. Scott noted that one of the
earliest urban renewal efforts helped fund the construction of the bridge on 2nd East,
which removed a significant infrastructure barrier and helped facilitate commercial
development, including the arrival of major retailers such as Albertsons and Walmart.
Dan Hanna referenced the University Boulevard/Yellowstone Highway area and noted
the importance of infrastructure improvements associated with growth near the high
school and surrounding development. Scott Miller continued discussing the University
Boulevard District and explained how urban renewal funding had assisted with
transportation improvements, utility relocations, canal modifications, and other
extraordinary infrastructure costs needed to support development.
Scott Miller then reviewed the North Interchange District, discussing growth in the area
surrounding the new LDS Temple, Home Depot, WinCo, and other commercial
developments. He also highlighted projects planned within the North Central District and

described how urban renewal funding has helped prepare infrastructure needed to
support future growth and development throughout those areas.
Scott Miller concluded by reiterating that urban renewal does not create a new tax. He
explained that the program reallocates a portion of future tax revenue growth generated
within the district and reinvests those funds into public infrastructure and eligible projects
within the same area. He noted that there is often confusion regarding the program and
emphasized that tax increment financing serves as a tool to encourage economic
development by helping fund improvements that might otherwise be cost-prohibitive.
Chairman McKay Francis asked how the Urban Renewal Agency collaborates with the
City and residents when determining which projects should be prioritized. He used the
example of potential future crossings over Highway 20 and asked how decisions are
made regarding which infrastructure needs receive attention and funding.
Scott Miller explained that project priorities are driven by ongoing evaluations of
community needs and development activity throughout the city. He noted that while the
Urban Renewal Agency has its own governing board that makes final decisions, city staff
and administrators provide recommendations and information regarding projects that
would have the greatest benefit to the community. Scott stated that Urban Renewal
works closely with City departments, particularly Public Works, to identify infrastructure
needs and determine where available resources can have the most impact.
Alan Parkinson referenced sewer infrastructure as an example of the collaboration
between the City and the Urban Renewal Agency. Scott Miller explained that the City
regularly identifies key infrastructure projects and works with Urban Renewal to
determine whether tax increment financing can be used to assist with funding. He cited a
sewer line project within the North Interchange District as an example of Urban Renewal
helping provide infrastructure necessary to support future development.
Vice Chair Tammy Geddes asked about the number of Urban Renewal Districts
currently in Rexburg, noting that the proposed district would become the fifth district, and
inquired about any limitations on the number of districts that can be created and how
they affect the allocation of tax revenues.
Scott Miller responded that Idaho law limits the total taxable base value contained
within all Urban Renewal Districts to no more than ten percent of the municipality's value
at the time the districts are established. He explained that although the districts appear
to cover a large geographic area, their original base values represented a much smaller
portion of the City's overall valuation when they were created. Scott also discussed the
lifespan of the existing districts, noting that the Downtown Urban Renewal District is
scheduled to expire in 2027, with funding for projects continuing through 2028 as the
district is brought to a close.
Megan Conrad provided additional clarification regarding the impact of urban renewal
districts on overlapping taxing entities. She explained that revenue allocation areas do
not take existing dollars away from taxing districts. Instead, urban renewal may
temporarily delay the ability of taxing districts to recognize additional budget capacity
generated from new construction until the district expires. Megan emphasized that the

process does not remove funding from the affected entities but rather delays the
distribution of a portion of future growth in tax revenues.
Chairman McKay Francis referenced information contained in the report and noted that
the statutory ten percent limit for urban renewal districts equated to approximately $273
million in taxable value. He observed that, including the proposed West Highway District,
the combined total would be approximately $115 million and asked if that figure was
accurate.
Scott Miller confirmed that the estimate appeared accurate and then reviewed the
anticipated expiration dates of the City's existing urban renewal districts. He explained
that the University Boulevard District is expected to expire around 2032, while the North
Interchange and North Central Districts still have many years remaining before reaching
their statutory expiration dates.
Chairman McKay Francis asked for clarification regarding the expiration of the
Downtown Urban Renewal District. Scott Miller confirmed that the district is nearing the
end of its lifespan and explained that, although the district officially expires in 2027,
project funding and closeout activities are expected to continue through 2028.
Tina Lesh asked whether urban renewal districts are generally established for twenty-
year terms. Scott Miller responded that current Idaho law limits new urban renewal
districts to twenty years. He noted that some older districts, including the Downtown
District, were created under previous regulations that allowed for longer durations.
Candi McFadden asked how the proposed district could affect property owners located
adjacent to, but outside of, the district boundaries. Scott Miller explained that urban
renewal districts do not directly increase taxes for neighboring properties. However, he
noted that infrastructure improvements funded through the district can benefit
surrounding areas and may positively affect property values. He further explained that
any impact on tax rates would be indirect and dependent upon factors such as property
valuation changes and decisions made by individual taxing entities.
Megan Conrad added that revenue allocation areas are generally considered a tax-
neutral mechanism. She explained that changes in property values and levy rates are
influenced by a variety of factors, making it difficult to directly attribute any future tax
changes to the existence of an urban renewal district. Megan agreed that the district
itself does not create a direct tax increase for neighboring property owners.
Scott Miller asked if Candi's question had been adequately answered. After receiving
confirmation, Chairman McKay Francis directed the meeting back to the agenda item,
and the presentation continued.

Brad Kramer explained how the proposed West Highway Urban Renewal District
supports the goals of the City's Comprehensive Plan. He discussed planned
infrastructure improvements, including road widening, intersection upgrades, sewer
improvements, sidewalks, and pedestrian connections needed to accommodate future
growth.
Brad Kramer stated that these projects would improve safety, connectivity, accessibility,
and economic development opportunities while supporting the City's long-term vision. He
concluded that the proposed Urban Renewal Plan was consistent with the
Comprehensive Plan and reminded the Commission that its role was to determine
whether it agreed with that finding. Brad then opened the floor for additional questions.

Commissioner Discussion:

Brian Thackeray asked what would happen if property values did not increase and the
district experienced economic hardship after being established. Brad Kramer explained
that if property values and development activity do not increase, no tax increment would
be generated, resulting in little or no funding being available for district projects. He
noted that urban renewal districts are typically created in areas where growth and
development are anticipated and acknowledged that there is some risk if expected
development does not occur.
Vern Muir asked what happens to the tax revenues associated with a district once it
reaches its expiration date, using the Downtown Urban Renewal District as an example.
Brad Kramer explained that any unspent funds remaining in the district are returned to
the county and redistributed among the appropriate taxing entities. He further stated that
once a district expires, all future tax revenues generated within the area are distributed
to the taxing entities as they would have been prior to the creation of the district.
Megan Conrad provided additional clarification regarding the expiration of urban
renewal districts. She explained that the increment value is merged back into the base
value when a district terminates, allowing the full taxable value to be recognized by the
affected taxing entities. She noted that this process can increase budget capacity and
affect future levy calculations for those entities.
Tina Lesh asked whether Urban Renewal Agencies can apply for federal grants. Alan
Parkinson responded that urban renewal can support grant applications and help
facilitate projects, but grant applications are typically submitted by the City or other
entities rather than the Urban Renewal Agency itself.
Scott Miller explained that the Urban Renewal Agency does not typically apply directly
for grants. He stated that grant applications are usually coordinated through the City or
through project partners, although urban renewal funding may play a supporting role in
those projects.
Brad Kramer added that he has worked on several projects where urban renewal
agencies partnered with grant-funded efforts. He explained that while urban renewal
agencies are generally not the direct applicants, they can serve as valuable partners by
contributing funding or resources that strengthen grant applications and help make
projects feasible.
Chairman McKay Francis asked what specific improvements residents could expect to
see from the funds generated through the proposed district. Brad Kramer explained that
the West Highway Urban Renewal Plan contains a list of priority projects and other
eligible improvements identified by the City and Urban Renewal Agency. He noted that

these projects would be funded as revenue becomes available and could be adjusted as
needs change over time.
Scott Miller highlighted the intersection of 12th West and Highway 33 as one of the
priority projects identified for future improvement. He explained that while it will take time
for tax increment revenues to accumulate, the Urban Renewal Agency has the ability to
issue bonds to begin infrastructure projects before all of the revenue has been collected.
Chairman McKay Francis asked how bonding would work in conjunction with future
district revenues. Scott Miller explained that any bond issued by the Urban Renewal
Agency would not be a levy bond or create a new tax. Instead, future tax increment
revenues generated within the district could be used to repay the bond, allowing critical
infrastructure projects to begin sooner while the district continues to develop.
Vern Muir asked for additional clarification regarding some of the projects listed in the
plan, specifically the proposed improvements at the 7th South intersection. Alan
Parkinson explained that the project involved improvements near 7th South and 12th
West and did not include extending the roadway across Highway 20. He noted that
improvements crossing the highway would involve additional coordination with state
agencies and funding sources but could potentially receive support from Urban Renewal
in the future.
Alan Parkinson provided examples of projects that have benefited from Urban Renewal
assistance, including utility relocations, traffic signal improvements, and major sewer
infrastructure projects. He discussed the planned signal near Home Depot, utility
improvements associated with development projects, and the relocation of the canal
along 12th West to accommodate future roadway widening. Alan explained that Urban
Renewal helps address extraordinary infrastructure costs that would otherwise create
barriers to development.
Vice Chair Tammy Geddes asked whether Urban Renewal becomes involved when the
City identifies a need for infrastructure improvements related to development. Alan
Parkinson explained that both the City and private developers can approach the Urban
Renewal Agency with potential projects. Tammy also asked how project coordination
occurs between the various entities involved.
Scott Miller confirmed that coordination regularly occurs between the Urban Renewal
Agency, City departments, and developers. He explained that Urban Renewal has its
own board that serves as the decision-making body, while City staff provide information
and recommendations regarding community needs and project priorities.
Brian Thackeray then summarized the purpose of the Commission's review and asked
whether the Commission's responsibility was simply to determine if the proposed Urban
Renewal District was consistent with the City's Comprehensive Plan. Scott Miller
confirmed that this was the Commission's role in the review process.
Dan Hanna asked whether urban renewal districts generate enough revenue to fund
their own projects. Scott Miller responded that they do and discussed several projects
that have benefited from urban renewal funding over the years, including bridges, sewer
infrastructure, and other community improvements. He also noted that urban renewal
had previously assisted with fire station improvements, although changes in state
regulations have made that type of funding more difficult today.
McKay Francis asked whether it was merely coincidental that a district with an
approximate value of $33 million was nearing expiration while a proposed district of a
similar size was being created. Scott Miller explained that it was not a specific target,
but that the timing made sense given the City's available capacity and the need to

added that the increased demand and development occurring within the proposed

McKay Francis then asked about the Commission's role in the process and whether its
recommendation would be forwarded to the City Council for final consideration. Scott
Miller explained that the next step would be a public hearing before the City Council,
currently anticipated for November 18, 2026.
Brad Kramer outlined the remaining approval process, explaining that if the
Commission found the proposed district consistent with the City's Comprehensive Plan,
additional public notice requirements and statutory procedures would still need to be
completed before the City Council could take final action.
Megan Conrad further explained that the City Council public hearing would be the final
major step in the process. She noted that notice of the hearing would be published,
affected taxing entities would receive copies of the plan, and additional agreements and
post-adoption filings would be completed if the district were approved.
Brad Kramer concluded by emphasizing the importance of completing the approval
process before the end of the calendar year. He explained that establishing the district
before year-end would preserve the intended base property values and allow the district
to function as planned once created.
Dan Hanna asked for clarification on the proper language needed for the motion
regarding the proposed Urban Renewal District. Scott Miller deferred the question to

Tina Lesh asked whether she should recuse herself from voting because she owns

that the City Attorney would be the appropriate person to provide a formal legal opinion.
A discussion followed regarding property ownership within the district, with Chairman

commissioner could make a fair and impartial decision. Following discussion, the
Commission determined that no conflict existed that would require recusal, and the
Commissioners remained eligible to vote on the item.
Brian Thackeray attempted to formulate a motion and proposed recommending
