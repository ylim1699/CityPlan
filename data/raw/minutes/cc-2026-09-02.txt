Meeting —
Mayor Merrill said he asked City Attorney Rammell to review proper meeting procedures training
before the meeting began.
City Attorney Rammell explained that maintaining order and decorum during City Council meetings
is important because it demonstrates professionalism and organization. He also noted that following
proper procedures assisted staff with preparing accurate meeting minutes. By clearly identifying
speakers before they speak makes it easier for city staff and AI -assisted transcription programs to
determine who is speaking.
City Attorney Rammell explained that, under Idaho Code, the mayor is the presiding Officer over
the City Council meetings and establishes the order of business subject to rules prescribed by state code.
He emphasized that the councilmembers should be recognized by the presiding officer before speaking.
For example, a councilmember with a question should first address the mayor and request recognition.
The mayor could then identify the councilmember before allowing them to proceed.
City Attorney Rammell explained that this procedure provides greater clarity in the official record
and reduces the amount of time staff spends manually identifying speakers while preparing minutes.
He said individuals participating in City Council meetings provided "comment" rather than
"testimony." Testimony generally involves individuals being sworn under oath, which is not the City's
practice. Therefore, references should be made to public comment, Council comment, or Council
deliberation rather than public testimony.
City Attorney Rammell stated that the City Council Handbook contained rules of procedure and
indicated that a few provisions need to be updated. He said City Clerk Lovejoy would distribute the
applicable section to council members as a refresher. The procedures addressed matters such as the
order of debate, including allowing the maker of a motion to speak first and giving priority to members
who have not yet spoken. He explained that these procedures helped the presiding officer maintain
balance and prevent one individual from dominating the discussion.
City Attorney Rammell also asked councilmembers not to entertain comments or questions called
out from the audience. He explained that this concern occurred more frequently during Planning and
Zoning public hearings and could create confusion and make it difficult to maintain an accurate official
record. He emphasized that maintaining a clear record is important if a governing body's decision later
^` became subject to litigation.
City Attorney Rammell said individuals wishing to speak should come forward, provide their name
and address, and present their question or comment through the appropriate process. Once a public
hearing is closed, additional public comments should not be accepted as part of the hearing. He noted
that staff remained willing to meet with individuals afterward to address questions or concerns.
City Attorney Rammell summarized three primary expectations for meeting procedures:
councilmembers should be recognized by the mayor or presiding officer before speaking; participants
should refer to statements from the public as comments rather than testimony; and comments or
questions called out from the audience should not be entertained.

A. Review of the West Highway Urban Renewal District Plan
Megan Conrad said she is an attorney with Elam & Burke in Boise who serves as legal counsel for the
Urban Renewal Agency. She explained that she drafted the proposed Urban Renewal Plan for the West
Highway District. The plan remains in draft form. She explained that the purpose of reviewing the plan
at this stage is to identify and address questions or concerns before beginning the formal plan approval
process and public meetings. Addressing substantive changes early in the process will make the
subsequent approval process more efficient.
Ms. Conrad noted that the Councilmembers and Commissioners might have received drafts
containing comments or other preliminary information. She stated that additional revisions are
expected as the boundaries of the revenue allocation area and the scope of proposed projects are
finalized. The plan narrative will also be updated to accurately reflect the economic feasibility study,
identified projects, and information regarding existing conditions from the eligibility study adopted in
2025.
Ms. Conrad stated that although additional revisions are anticipated, the overall substance,
framework, scope, and purpose of the proposed plan have been substantially developed. The meeting
provides an opportunity for the City Council and County Commissioners to provide feedback and ask
questions before the formal approval process begins. She explained that the goal is to complete the
required process and bring the plan before the City Council for consideration through an ordinance,
with the proposed district established on or before December 31, 2026.

Ms. Conrad reviewed the taxing districts that overlapped many of the county parcels. The districts are
like those associated with the city parcels, with some differences, including the fire district. Consistent
with the previous discussion, the revenue model assumed that no revenue will be generated from the
fire district levy, which was reflected as zero in the projections. From a legal perspective, she explained
that Idaho Code § 50-2905 identified the information required to be included in an urban renewal plan.

Legislative changes also required that this information be provided with greater specificity. The plan
addressed eight statutory factors and identified where the supporting information can be found within
the plan or its attachments.
Ms. Conrad explained that the economic feasibility study, identified as Attachment 5, is a primary
component of the revenue allocation area. The study identified the proposed projects, projected revenue
generation, and anticipated cash flow to demonstrate the overall feasibility of the plan.
The first statutory factor addressed the total assessed valuation of the base assessment roll for the
revenue allocation area compared with the total assessed valuation of taxable property within the
go" municipality. This requirement was referred to as the "1o% test." The combined base assessment roll of
all existing revenue allocation areas could not exceed 1o% of the city's total taxable value. Because the
proposed area included both city and county parcels, the analysis conservatively used the City's total
taxable value because it represented the smaller value.
The second factor required a statement identifying the type, number, and location of proposed public
works or improvements. This information is included in Attachment 5 and Section 301 of the plan
narrative. Section 301 remained under review to ensure that its narrative accurately reflected the intent
and purpose of the projects identified in the economic feasibility study. The detailed estimated project
costs are also provided in Attachment 5. The fiscal impact statement addressed the effect of the revenue
allocation area on overlapping taxing districts before and after repayment of any bonds. This
information is included in Section 502.8 of the plan and in the economic feasibility study. The primary
impact on overlapping taxing districts involved a deferred benefit from increases in taxable value
occurring within the revenue allocation area. Growth within the area would not be available to increase
the taxing districts' budget capacity from new construction until the revenue allocation area terminated.
Upon termination, 8o% of the increment value would flow to the new construction roll for purposes of
increasing budget capacity.
Ms. Conrad also reviewed the methods available to finance project costs. These included a pay-as-
you-go approach in which funds are accumulated over time, advance funding by developers, property
owners, or other public or private third parties with reimbursement by the Agency for eligible projects
under a reimbursement agreement, and the issuance of Agency debt to advance projects. Any bond
issuance would be required to satisfy applicable lender and financial requirements. All these financing
methods were contemplated within the plan. The plan's duration was established at 20 years. If the plan
is established by the end of the year, the termination date will be December 31, 2046, with the agency
eligible to receive its final year of revenue in 2047. The termination provisions are addressed in Section
Boo of the plan. She further explained that any assets owned or retained by the agency at termination
can be conveyed as provided in Section Boo. The agency can also retain assets after termination if
funding sources other than revenue allocation proceeds are available to support those assets.

Brad Kramer with Perspective Planning Consulting presented an overview of the Economic
Feasibility Study he prepared. He explained that the purpose of the study was to determine whether tax
increment revenue generated from anticipated private investment would be sufficient to cover the costs
of proposed public improvements and other eligible projects within the revenue allocation area. He said
he evaluated vacant properties within the proposed area and worked with the city to estimate
anticipated development, future property values, and growth over the life of the district as part of the
study. The analysis also considered historical levy rates, changes in property valuations, proposed
public projects, and estimated project costs.
Mr. Kramer reported that the proposed revenue allocation area is economically feasible. Projected tax
increment revenues are anticipated to fund at least the priority projects, as well as many of the
additional projects identified within the district. The study evaluated several development scenarios to
account for uncertainty in future economic conditions, construction costs, and market demand.
Although the city initially anticipated a io-year buildout of the vacant properties, a more conservative
analysis was conducted because the Agency anticipated issuing a bond to fund some of the public
infrastructure. The study evaluated io-year, 12-year,15-year, and 20-year buildout scenarios to
determine how slower development could affect bond repayment, revenues, and expenditures.

Mr. Kramer concluded that the projected cash flow supported the economic feasibility of the
proposed revenue allocation area and offered to provide additional detail or answer questions regarding

County Commissioner Smith asked whether the agency anticipated bonding for the entire $27.3
million in priority projects or for a smaller amount. Mr. Kramer clarified that the economic feasibility
model assumed a bond of approximately $6 million rather than bonding for the full cost of the priority
projects. The remaining project costs are anticipated to be funded through revenue generated over the
life of the district. The bond is intended to provide initial funding to begin priority projects without
waiting for sufficient revenue to accumulate.
Council Member Johnson asked for clarification regarding the proposed bond and whether the city
or agency has used similar financing for other urban renewal districts. Mayor Merrill explained that
bonding has been used previously, including within the urban renewal district near Walmart. Bonds
provided upfront funding needed to begin infrastructure improvements rather than waiting for revenue
to accumulate over time. He explained that requiring individual businesses or developers to pay the
entire cost of infrastructure that benefited a broader area could discourage development because of the
significant additional expense. Bond financing allowed infrastructure costs that benefited the overall
district to be funded more broadly. Mayor Merrill also clarified that the proposed bond would not be a
general obligation bond and would not require an election. Instead, the agency will issue the bond to an
interested purchaser and repay the obligation through available revenue.
Mayor Merrill addressed concerns that cities and counties could lose tax revenue for the entire 20-
year life of an urban renewal district. Although a district could remain in place for 20 years,
^ development and major projects did not typically occur immediately after the district was established.
In many cases, significant development occurred during the middle or later years of the district. He
referenced the downtown urban renewal district as an example, noting that efforts are underway to
complete a major project before the district terminated. Because the project will occur near the end of
the district's life, the increased taxable value generated by the project will remain within the revenue
allocation area for only a short period before becoming available to the overlapping taxing districts.
The actual period during which taxing districts experienced a deferred benefit from new development is
often considerably less than 20 years. Depending on when development occurs, the period can be
approximately six, eight, ten, or sometimes 15 to 16 years. Larger projects often occur during the middle
or later years of an urban renewal district.

Ms. Conrad provided a final update regarding agricultural property consents and explained that the
remaining consents would determine the final boundaries of the proposed revenue allocation area. They
will continue to pursue the remaining agricultural property owner's consents, and any parcels for which
consent is not obtained will be removed from the final map.
Ms. Conrad said the proposed plan is subject to statutory provisions applicable to plans established
after July 1, 2016, including the base reset provisions. If the City Council subsequently approved certain
modifications to the plan by ordinance, the base value could reset to the district's current equalized
assessed value. Such a reset could significantly reduce or eliminate the district's revenue stream.
Ms. Conrad noted that statutory exceptions existed for actions that were not considered plan
modifications for purposes of the base reset. These included a one-time plan amendment to add area,
de -annexations, ministerial amendments, and certain expansions of existing commercial or industrial
projects. She added that the agency's annual budgeting relied on assumptions regarding levy rates and
projected revenues. Actual levy rates and revenues could fluctuate from year to year, requiring some
flexibility in the Agency's budget and project schedule.
Council President E. Erickson asked for clarification regarding the base reset provisions and
whether modifications made during the 20-year life of the plan could cause the base value to reset to
the current assessed valuation. Ms. Conrad confirmed that a plan amendment formally processed and
approved by City Council ordinance could trigger a base reset unless the amendment qualified under
one of the statutory exceptions.
Council President E. Erickson asked whether this significantly limited the ability to expand the
district in the future. Ms. Conrad explained that one exception allowed a one-time amendment to add
property totaling up to io% of the existing revenue allocation area's acreage without triggering a base
reset. For example, if the district remained approximately 84o acres, the boundary could be expanded
one-time by up to approximately 84 acres. This one-time expansion would fall within the statutory
exception and would not trigger a base reset. However, a second amendment to add additional area
could trigger a base reset.
Commissioner Mendenhall asked for clarification regarding the transfer of powers ordinance and
the intergovernmental agreement. Ms. Conrad explained that the transfer of powers ordinance
generally acknowledged that tax increment revenue generated from county parcels within the revenue
allocation area will be allocated to the Urban Renewal Agency for implementation of the plan. The
intergovernmental agreement similarly recognized the multijurisdictional nature of the plan and
established an understanding between the city and county regarding administration of the revenue
allocation area. The Agency Board will administer the plan, and increment revenue will flow to the
agency and be used in accordance with the approved plan. She noted that these requirements are
addressed in Idaho Code § 50-2906.
Commissioner Smith clarified that the transfer of powers and intergovernmental agreement related
to the allocation of revenue and did not transfer land use or zoning authority. Ms. Conrad confirmed
that the revenue allocation area will not change existing land use, zoning, permitting, or development
requirements. Property owners will continue to follow the applicable jurisdiction's standard permitting,
design, and development processes. Agricultural property will also continue to be valued as agricultural

easier. Ms. Conrad explained that the revenue allocation area has no effect on the city's authority or

Scott Miller provided additional context regarding the location of the county properties on the

1. Resolution No 2026 - 12 Amend Eagle Park Camping Use Policy Action Item
Recreation Director Lewis reported that the city has received several concerning reports over the
previous few months regarding interactions with individuals at Eagle Park. After spending additional
time monitoring the park, he determined that operational changes are needed to improve public safety.
He noted that several individuals indicated that they did not feel comfortable walking through the park
alone and emphasized that city parks should feel safe and accessible to the public.
Recreation Director Lewis recommended changing the camping procedures at Eagle Park by
limiting camping to Friday, Saturday, and Sunday rather than allowing camping throughout the week.
He explained that the change would make it easier for the Police Department and city staff to monitor
the park Campers will be required to visibly display their permits so staff can more easily identify
individuals who are authorized to camp. He proposed changes intended to improve safety and allow the
city to monitor the park more effectively without requiring significant additional resources.
Council Member Johnson explained that she had discussed the proposed changes with Recreation
Director Lewis and that the Parks Committee have reviewed the issue. She believes the proposal
provided a reasonable balance between allowing residents to continue using Eagle Park and
establishing restrictions to address ongoing safety concerns. She noted that the secluded nature of the
park has contributed to some of the problems. Mayor Merrill agreed that the proposal provided an
appropriate balance, noting that the city did not want to be overly restrictive but needed to ensure the
park remained safe for the public.
Recreation Director Lewis explained that the proposed restrictions are not necessarily intended to
be permanent. As conditions at Eagle Park improved and the city developed better procedures for
managing and monitoring the area, some restrictions could potentially be reduced. He stated that the
proposed measures are necessary at that time to address the existing concerns.
Council President E. Erickson asked whether the camping permit requirements and payment
process at Eagle Park are clearly identified through signage. Recreation Director Lewis stated that the
existing signage did not clearly reflect the proposed requirements but will be updated if the City Council
approves the changes.
Recreation Director Lewis explained that an electronic payment and registration system is already
available. The proposed process is intended to require campers to check in with the city because the
am existing honor system has resulted in relatively few campers paying the required fee. He stated that a
more formal permitting process will also allow city staff to track activity within the park and address
safety concerns.
Recreation Director Lewis reported that city staff and the Police Department have recently
responded to a group that remained at the park longer than permitted and accumulated numerous
belongings throughout the area. He believes the proposed permitting requirements will provide greater
control over camping activity and improve safety.
Mayor Merrill asked about allowing campers to register and pay online, including the possibility of
placing a QR code on park signage. Recreation Director Lewis explained that the permit would need to
13

having to contact each patron. Campers could print the permit themselves or obtain a printed copy
from City Hall.

Recreation Director Lewis explained that the registration system generally requires reservations to be

channels.

B. Finance: -Matt Nielson
1. Review and approve Fire Department Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Action Item
Finance Officer Nielson provided background on the joint venture agreement used to fund fire and
ambulance services within Madison County. He explained that three taxing entities contributed to the
agreement: the Ambulance District, which is funded through property taxes paid by all County
15

residents; the Fire District, which is funded by residents outside the City of Rexburg; and the City of
Rexburg's General Fund, which provides the city's proportionate share of fire service costs primarily
through property and sales tax revenues. A Fire Board, consisting of representatives from each entity,
administer the joint venture agreement.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the Joint Powers Agreement (JPA) budget represented the
operating budget for fire and ambulance services. Additional funds are maintained for equipment
replacement, wildland operations, and other purposes. The original proposed subsidy for the three
entities totaled approximately $6.69 million, with the city's fire portion at approximately $2.2 million,
which had been included in the city's adopted budget. During subsequent budget work meetings,
several adjustments were proposed, including a new fuels reduction and shift coverage position. The
position will assist with fuels reduction activities primarily during the summer months and provide
shift coverage during the remainder of the year. Approximately $48,000 of the position's cost will be
covered by a federal grant.
Finance Officer Nielson reported that the city requested detailed line -item budgeting for the
Wildland Fund to more accurately account for revenues and expenses. The Fire Board supported the
request, and Chief Troy Miskin provided a revised proposal on approximately August 26, 2026. Finance
Officer Nielson stated that the revised budget provides greater detail but identified several additional
adjustments that are needed. Expenses transferred to the Wildland Fund have not been removed from
the operating budget, resulting in duplication. He recommended reducing the operating budget
accordingly.
Finance Officer Nielson also recommended properly accounting for equipment reimbursements
received from wildland deployments. He explained that when jointly owned fire equipment was
deployed, reimbursement revenues were received and ultimately distributed to the appropriate fire
equipment replacement fund to help fund future equipment purchases. He reviewed his
recommendations with Chief Child and Chief Miskin, who considered the proposed adjustments
reasonable. He also identified accounting changes that he anticipated will be addressed during the
department's audit. If the recommended adjustments are approved by the Fire Board, the overall
subsidy from Fiscal Year 2026 to Fiscal Year 2027 would decrease by approximately $82,000, primarily
because of additional ambulance revenue. The Fire District's contribution will decrease by
approximately $51,000, while the city's fire contribution will decrease by approximately $71,000 from
the previously proposed amount. The city's fire budget will still represent approximately a 3.3%
increase. The adjustments will also allow approximately $15,000 to be restored to the city's General
Fund contingency.
Council Member Reeser asked how the city's share of the joint venture costs is determined, noting
that the city appeared to pay approximately one-third of the costs. He asked whether the allocation is
based on the three participating entities, the number of households, or the amount of revenue
generated by each taxing entity.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the joint venture agreement established different cost -
sharing formulas depending on the type of expense. Building improvements at the downtown facility
are divided equally among the City, Fire District, and Ambulance District, with each entity paying one-
third. Fire equipment costs are divided equally between the City and Fire District, while the Ambulance
District does not contribute to those expenses. Ambulance equipment is funded entirely by the
Ambulance District.
Finance Officer Nielson further explained that personnel costs, which represent one of the largest
expenses, are allocated differently. The Ambulance District pays 48% of personnel costs, while the
remaining 52% is divided between the Fire District and the city. The allocation between the Fire District
and city is calculated using a formula that considers both assessed property values and population
within each jurisdiction. He noted that determining the final allocations requires an extensive
spreadsheet with multiple calculations.
Council Member C. Erickson expressed support for establishing a defined budget and emphasized
the importance of operating within the available funding. He stated that the city does not have
unlimited resources and cannot continue providing additional funding without established limits. He
appreciated that the proposed approach clearly identified a budget, encouraged cost savings, and
provided the city with a better understanding of its financial responsibility.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that a new budget addendum had been drafted to establish a
joint operating fund. Under the proposed approach, the budgeted contributions will be placed into the
joint operating fund. If funds remain at the end of the year due to effective financial management, the
remaining balance will carry over and remain within the budget. He noted that this approach
encouraged responsible use of the funds and allowed carryover funds to be used for future needs, such
as adding positions or addressing other expenses. From the city's budgeting perspective, they closely
monitor expenditures and percentage increases based on anticipated General Fund revenues. This
approach allowed the city to maintain greater control over its financial contribution.

i. Local Improvement District (LID) Committee Report
Public Works Director Davidson presented the recommendations from the Local Improvement
District (LID) Committee. He reported that the committee had met six times since January. The
committee initially reviewed background information regarding street funding and the various revenue
sources available to the city. Committee members then brainstormed potential funding options,
evaluated the advantages and disadvantages of each option, and considered possible revenue sources.
The committee ultimately developed a presentation and completed its final review on August 6.
The committee considered several funding options, including a permanent levy override, petitioning the
State for an increase in the fuel tax, pursuing a local option sales tax, adjusting the frequency or cost -
sharing structure of LIDS, increasing the budget allocation for street reconstruction, and exploring
alternative approaches for sidewalk replacement. He acknowledged that some options would require
significant time and effort to implement but stated that the committee wanted to consider all potential
funding sources.
Council Member Tietjen asked Public Workds Director Davidson to provide an overview of the LID
,am process. He explained that the city primarily uses LIDS in conjunction with street reconstruction
projects to replace sidewalks, curbs, gutters, and, when necessary, sewer connections to bring the area
up to current standards. He noted that the City Council recently approved the city assuming the cost of
curb and gutter replacement, while property owners remained responsible for sidewalk improvements
and necessary sewer connection replacements.
Public Works Director Davidson explained that reconstructed streets might not receive another
major reconstruction for approximately 30 to 50 years. For that reason, the city evaluated sewer
connections during reconstruction projects to help ensure they would last for the expected life of the
new roadway and avoid having to excavate a newly reconstructed street to repair a failed sewer line. An
LID allowes the city to obtain financing for eligible improvements so property owners are not required
to pay the entire cost upfront. Instead, property owners could repay their portion of the improvements
17

over a io-year period. He acknowledged that while the financing option benefited property owners,
sidewalk replacement costs could still be difficult for residents because they are generally not expenses
homeowners anticipated or are eager to incur.
Public Works Director Davidson mentioned an additional street funding option that he had
recently learned about while attending an Idaho Transportation Department meeting. He explained
that counties could seek voter approval to increase annual vehicle registration fees, with the additional
revenue used to support street -related expenses. The amount of the additional registration fee would be
established through the ballot measure. For example, an additional $35 registration fee could be
proposed, and the potential revenue generated could then be calculated based on the number of
registered vehicles.

Mayor Merrill reported that he and Public Works Director Davidson participated in the Idaho Water
Users Association's annual Water College, which the city hosted at City Hall. He explained that the
statewide educational event is designed to help current and prospective legislators better understand
water -related issues. Public Works Director Davidson and representatives from Rural Water and other
organizations provided presentations regarding water challenges and opportunities to improve water
management throughout the state. Following the presentations, participants toured several water
projects. The group visited Walters Pond to observe the City's groundwater recharge efforts and then
traveled through the St. Anthony and Ashton areas to view irrigation improvement projects that have
received State funding.
Mayor Merrill highlighted the use of automated canal headgates as an example of improved water
efficiency. He explained that water managers have traditionally been required to manually remove
boards from canal structures to control water flow. New automated stainless -steel gates allowed
operators to remotely open and close the gates using electronic controls. The previous manual process
could take several days and potentially limit opportunities for groundwater recharge. The automated
system allowed water managers to respond more quickly and efficiently when water became available
for recharge, including directing water toward the Egin Lakes area near the sand dunes where it could
infiltrate into the aquifer. He emphasized the importance of continuing to improve water efficiency and
management practices. He also expressed concern about future water supplies if the region did not
receive adequate mountain snowfall during the upcoming winter.

B. Review committee recommendation of Police Station Facility options — Matt Nielson & Josh
Rhodes Action Item
Finance Officer Nielson introduced the Police Station Options Committee discussion and explained
that he and Police Chief Josh Rhodes would jointly present the information. He stated that the internal
committee included the Mayor, Council Member Reeser, the City Attorney, the CFO, the Police Chief,
the Assistant Police Chief, and several consultants who participated in one of the meetings.
Police Chief Rhodes thanked the City Council and City staff for their continued support in evaluating
options for the Police Department. He explained that continued residential and commercial growth
increased the need for a facility capable of accommodating the department's current and future
operational needs.
Chief Rhodes identified evidence storage as one of the department's critical concerns. Evidence was
stored in the basement of the existing facility, where available space had become increasingly limited.
The department has begun relocating some evidence to its secondary training facility, but storage needs
continue to increase as call volumes, cases, and evidence processing grow. He mentioned technology
and facility limitations. He stated that the department faced ongoing financial challenges associated
with maintaining and updating cameras and other technology. Detectives shared office space, which
created privacy and scheduling challenges when conducting investigations and interviewing victims and
witnesses. The department also lacked dedicated victim and witness interview rooms and instead used a
hard interview room and a multipurpose conference room. Larger cases involving multiple suspects,
victims, or witnesses sometimes required the department to use other facilities for interviews.
Additional space concerns include office space, parking, fingerprinting services, and public service
areas. Chief Rhodes noted that during busy periods, particularly when students returned and requested
parking permits, approximately 30 to 5o people could be lined up outside the Police Department.
Fingerprinting services were also conducted within the crowded front lobby area. He emphasized that
these limitations would become more significant as the City and Police Department continued to grow
and add personnel.
Chief Rhodes reviewed the previous efforts to fund a new police facility through general obligation
bonds. He stated that bond measures had been presented to voters in November 2025 and again in May
2026, and both measures had failed. Following the first bond election, the city conducted a community
survey and received feedback regarding concerns about project cost and location. The proposal was
subsequently modified to reduce costs and respond to public feedback. He said voter participation
increased during the second bond election while the percentage of affirmative votes decreased.
Following the second unsuccessful bond measure, the city formed the internal committee to evaluate
alternative options for addressing the Police Department's facility needs. He noted that location
continued to be one of the primary concerns identified through community feedback. The committee
subsequently began reviewing potential sites and alternatives throughout the city to determine an
appropriate path forward.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the committee identified three options for the City Council's
consideration. He clarified that these are not the only possible options and encouraged
Councilmembers to discuss any additional alternatives they believe should be considered. He stated the
PER committee focused on the three options they believe are the most realistic based on potential locations,
construction methods, and estimated costs. They analyzed possible down payments and whether
providing a down payment can reduce the amount the city will need to borrow.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that funding sources were also considered and will continue to be
an important part of the discussion. He noted that available funding significantly affects which options
are financially feasible. Although the most financially feasible option might not always be the preferred
option, financial considerations are an important factor in determining the best approach. They
narrowed the analysis to three primary options and will provide the City Council with an individual
analysis of each option.

Finance Officer Nielson explained that the committee identified three primary options for
addressing the Police Department's facility needs. The first option is to wait a few years and attempt to
pass another general obligation (GO) bond, which will require a supermajority vote, to construct a new
police station. The second option is to purchase an existing building in the city and renovate it,
potentially adding new space if the building is too small. The committee reviewed a couple of potential
sites, including the BMC property, although it is unknown whether the property is for sale or what the
purchase price would be. The third option is to construct a new City Hall office building and renovate
the existing City Hall for use as a police station. He noted that this option has been discussed by the City
Council periodically over the previous six years.
Finance Officer Nielson said regarding the first option, the city already owns approximately five
acres of land where a new police station could potentially be constructed. Although the property is not P`l
located downtown, it provided sufficient space for the Police Department's offices, training facilities,
and public gathering areas. A new facility could also address the Department's safety, security, and
technology concerns. Additionally, the building could be designed to accommodate future additions and
expansion.
Finance Officer Nielson said the primary challenges associated with option one is limited funding
sources and uncertain timing. The city will need to determine how much funding can be provided
upfront as a down payment and how much can be supported annually for debt payments. The city will
also need to return the proposal to voters, with no guarantee that a GO bond will pass. The city
previously evaluated alternative financing through a lease appropriation when considering placing a
new police station on the ballot. That analysis estimated that the General Fund would need to provide
more than $800,000 annually. They determined that the city could not financially support that level of
annual payment.

Finance Officer Nielson also explained that constructing a new City Hall provided additional
financing opportunities, including a lease appropriation purchase option and potentially a public -
private partnership. He indicated that the lease appropriation method is a proven financing model that
has been used for facilities such as the Idaho Falls Police Station and the Chubbuck City Hall and Police
Station. This financing method can be used by cities without a public vote and typically involves
financing over a period of approximately zo to 3o years. For the initial phase of converting the existing
City Hall into a police station. The committee identified three primary improvements that will need to
be addressed immediately: secure public entrance, adequate evidence storage space, and a secure police
parking area.
Police Chief added that much of the existing interior office space can accommodate necessary offices,
interview rooms, and other operational needs without extensive renovation, although security —
improvements will still be required. The existing City Hall is already equipped with security cameras
that can continue to be utilized. Existing office spaces can also be converted into interview rooms with
minimal modifications. The primary improvements will include installing appropriate cameras and
microphones in those rooms, allowing the existing space to meet the Police Department's interview
room needs.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the lease appropriation financing method is formally
referred to as a Certificate of Participation. Under this arrangement, the local government will own the
land while another entity will finance and develop the building. During the financing term, the city will
appropriate funds annually through its budget process to make the required lease payments. At the end
of the term, ownership of the building will be transferred to the city, making the arrangement like a
lease -to -own agreement. He explained that if the city decided at some point during the term that it no
longer needed the facility, it could choose not to appropriate the annual lease payment. The entity that
financed the building can then rent the facility for the remainder of the lease term, with the city
ultimately receiving ownership at the end of the term. This financing method is attractive to developers
and investors because there is a low likelihood that the city will discontinue the annual appropriation
once the facility is in use.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the committee considered why constructing a new City Hall
is being evaluated when the primary need is a new police facility. The committee determined that this
option could provide the greatest benefit to taxpayers because it allowed the city to use alternative
financing, such as a lease appropriation, and will likely avoid the need for a general obligation bond and
an associated property tax increase. He said a new City Hall will have a relatively small footprint
because the form -based code requires a multi -story building. The proposed location will not require
removing existing buildings from the tax rolls, will remain downtown near other public facilities such as
the library and tabernacle, and will be located near planned public parking. He noted that if Madison
County eventually constructed a facility on the adjacent property, additional governmental services
would be in the same area. Another significant advantage is the estimated construction cost. The cost
per square foot for a three-story office building is expected to be substantially lower than the estimated
cost of constructing a new police facility, potentially by approximately one-third. Option three also
provided the greatest number of potential funding sources for both the down payment and annual
payments.
Finance Officer Nielson reviewed potential funding sources available for the three options. These
included a general obligation bond, General Fund resources, funds previously reserved for a municipal
building, police impact fees, grants such as Community Development Block Grants (CDBG), private
contributions, and the City's Revolving Loan Fund (RLF). The city successfully used CDBG funding for
previous public facilities and parking improvements. Private contributions could also be considered,
particularly because individuals have previously expressed interest in contributing toward a new police
facility.
Finance Officer Nielson explained that the Revolving Loan Fund has also historically been used to
help finance municipal facilities, including the existing City Hall when it was remodeled. The fund
generated revenue through investments and loans to businesses, allowing the balance to grow over
time. He estimated that approximately $400,000 from the RLF could potentially be available toward a
project down payment. He then reviewed City departments and enterprise funds that pay overhead
costs for centralized City services. These included Building Safety, Engineering, Shop, Sanitation,
Water, Wastewater Treatment and Collections, GIS, Airport, and Golf. Centralized services included
administration, Human Resources, Information Technology, Customer Service, Finance, Legal, and
other governmental functions. Each year, a portion of the cost of providing these services is allocated to
the applicable departments through overhead charges.
Finance Officer Nielson said because these departments benefited from services provided through
City Hall, a portion of the cost of constructing and financing a new City Hall can be allocated to them.
The departments could contribute toward the initial down payment and could also pay a proportionate
share of annual financing costs through their overhead rates. As an example, Finance Officer Nielson
presented a scenario in which a new City Hall cost approximately $li million. If the city provided a $6
million down payment, approximately $5 million will need to be financed. Based on an analysis

financial tradeoffs associated with constructing a new facility using existing revenues.
Mayor Merrill noted that the existing City Hall was originally a JCPenney building and was
successfully renovated into a functional municipal facility. He believes the building could similarly be
renovated into a safe and functional police station. He acknowledged that renovations could cost several
million dollars but stated that the building's location and existing structure made it a viable option for
long-term police use.
Mayor Merrill expressed concern that delaying further evaluation will continue to postpone
improvements to the Police Department's facility. He supported evaluating additional alternatives but
recommended moving forward with the planning process to obtain more detailed information
ome regarding renovation costs, security improvements, and financing options.
Finance Officer Nielson clarified that the planning phase will determine the square footage needed
for a new City Hall and the estimated cost to renovate the existing City Hall into a police station. City
Staff will also identify potential funding sources for the down payment and ongoing annual payments.
The funding analysis will be applied to all three options without considering a general obligation bond.
City Staff will determine which funding sources can be used for each option and identify available cash
reserves, potential down payment amounts, the remaining amount that will need to be borrowed, and
the estimated annual lease appropriation payment. The analysis involves several variables and will take
time to complete. Mayor Merrill reiterated that entering the planning phase will not commit the city to
a specific option.
