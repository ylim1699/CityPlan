Public Works Director Davidson said the City Councilmembers received the official costs of LID
54 in their packets. Council President E. Erickson asked whether the bids came in considerably lower
than the original estimates. Public Works Director Davidson clarified that some bids came in higher,
but the costs to property owners decreased significantly because the city took over the cost of the curb
and gutter. The properties that only required sidewalk work did not experience the same reduction. He
confirmed that the city taking over the curb and gutter costs accounted for the difference.
Council Member Johnson asked about the letters that had been received and specifically questioned
whether the Goss property would be charged. Public Works Director Davidson explained that an
Orangeburg sewer pipe serving one of the homes required further review. The approximately $3,500
charge appeared to have been for replacement of that sewer line. He said additional research is needed
to determine exactly what occurred and city staff will follow up with the Gosses.
Public Works Director Davidson also clarified that no sidewalk work was completed on the
property because the property owners had changed the zoning of their property to HDR and had
anticipated selling the property. However, the Orangeburg sewer pipe needs to be addressed because
those pipes are known to fail, and city staff wants to avoid a pipe failure occurring in the interim.
Council Member Johnson asked about another letter concerning the Kitchell property and whether
the property had a curb. Public Works Director Davidson explained that there is existing curb, but it is
in poor condition. The property was considered one of the miscellaneous properties included in the LID
project, and the city did not plan to reconstruct the street in that area, so curb work was not included.
He provided additional background on the property. The property changed ownership around 2oig and
was refinanced, possibly in 2022. The refinancing brought the property to the city's attention because
the bank requested information regarding any outstanding obligations, including LIDS. City Staff
subsequently communicated with the property owners regarding the sidewalk requirement.
Public Works Director Davidson continued to explain that Salem Road is a narrow street with
some homes located close to the roadway similar to Bear Street, where sidewalks had been required.
Initially, the city had not required the property owner to install a sidewalk because a final
determination regarding sidewalks on those streets had not yet been made. After further analysis, the
city determined that sidewalks needed to be installed on those streets as properties changed ownership.
He said an LID letter had been sent to the property owners on February 20, 2025, followed by an email
regarding the sidewalk on April 23, 2025. A city intern also attempted to contact the property owners by
phone but apparently had not been able to reach them.

Presentation Automatic License Plate Recognition (ALPR) Camera Systems Safety — Taylor
Riley
Good evening, Councilmembers, tonight I'm not asking you to solve a crime. I'm asking you to decide
whether every innocent civilian in Rexburg should become part of a nationwide Al surveillance
network.
My name is Taylor Riley, and I appreciate the opportunity to discuss a topic impacting communities
across the country: AI surveillance systems. A month ago, I asked this council to take action by
establishing ordinances to prevent these technologies from coming to Rexburg. Tonight, I'll share
information and data to help you make an informed decision. This is a significant issue involving
privacy, constitutional rights, and responsible use of citizens' information. I'm an outdoorsman,
entrepreneur, and Idaho resident of 17 years. My wife and I chose this area as our long-term home,
and my background is in media production, marketing, and outdoor education. About two months
ago, I began researching AI surveillance after noticing an increase in traffic cameras and sensors
across cities, including small communities. I started hearing about systems being deployed
PER nationwide where vehicle data was collected and accessed without traditional search warrants or
probable cause. That caught my attention. So, let's dive in. What is all this Flock talk about?
Flock Safety began in 2017, as a privately held manufacturer and operator of security hardware and
software. It operates its systems under contracts with law enforcement agencies, cities, counties,
neighborhood associations and private property owners. Their product line consists of License Plate
Readers, Video Cameras, Audio Detection, Mobile Security Trailers, F1ockOS, and Drone as First
Responder. They claim that 1o,000+ missing people were located in 2o25, and 1 million plus cases
were supported by Flock last year. They sell the idea of safety, reduced crime, searchable evidence,
cross -agency visibility, audit -ready use, local control, and faster incident response.
Flock license plate readers are motion -activated cameras connected to cloud -based software that uses
AI to capture, analyze, and search vehicle data. These cameras are commonly installed at roads,
neighborhood entrances, and parking lot exits, collecting information such as license plates, vehicle
characteristics, and other identifying details. The process is simple: agencies contract with Flock,
install the cameras, and the collected data is processed through Flock's cloud -based platform. In
Idaho, police chiefs in Idaho Falls, Wilder, Greenleaf, and St. Anthony moved forward with Flock
systems without requiring city council approval before installation. This is St. Anthony's contract. The
city purchased F1ockOS, Flock's cloud -based platform that connects surveillance equipment and data
into one system. It uses AI to analyze data, provide alerts, enable cross -agency sharing, integrate
with CAD and records systems, and includes tools like Free Form Search. St. Anthony purchased four
Safety Bundles, which include solar powered license plate readers and video cameras connected to the
FlockOS platform. The installation cost was $17,000, with an annual subscription cost of $17,000. The
contract was signed last October by Dan Haley, Flock's Chief Legal Officer, and Chief Greg Belew of
..� the St. Anthony Police Department. The cameras were installed on Bridge Street and are maintained
by both the City of St. Anthony and Flock. The equipment is installed and is now part of a surveillance
system of over 125,o82 automated license plate readers, powered by AI, that captures and analyzes
images of all passing vehicles. It stores the details of your car's location, the date, and time. It
analyzes the make, model, color, and even features such as dents, roof racks, bumper stickers, all of
which become searchable data points. The cameras collect data on millions of vehicles regardless of
whether the driver is suspected of a crime. This data does not require a warrant to use. The network
produces more than 20 billion vehicle tracking plate scans every month. But Flock isn't just a license
plate reader. It has been deployed in parks and public spaces. Their website says that it's forparks of
all sizes. It boasts its Always -On Outdoor Security Cameras: 'Flock Video helps you prevent
vandalism, monitor behavior, and respond to complaints with confidence. Clear footage gives parks
teams and law enforcement what they need, fast."Its Gunshot Detection also goes beyond gunshots to

include other audio detection that is AI programmed to listen and record, including vehicle crashes,
fireworks, and protests or public demonstrations. Let's notforget to mention that Flock cameras use a
combination of motion detection and infrared technology, or IR. IR allows cameras to capture clear
images at night without using visible flashes to the human eye. We put all these features together, and
this is just one company, in a growing surveillance industry, seeking contracts with agencies,
businesses, schools, parks, and so on. Other companies such as Axon, Motorola Solutions, Genetec,
and Leonardo, Neology are competing for data collection and surveillance solutions as well. The
surveillance race is on. And it isn't showing signs of slowing down.
One other emerging technology is Leonardo's Signal Trace, which analyzes electronic devices that
travel together to create a unique digital fingerprint. Devices such as phones, smartwatches, fitness
trackers, your dog's microchip, and other devices emitting frequencies can be detected and paired with other surveillance systems, even without a known license plate. Signal Trace is effective in off -
road areas such as parks and stores and performs with or without license plate readers. Now, here's
what happens after all this data is collected. The data is uploaded to the nationwide networks.
Artificial intelligence automatically analyzes what is recorded and makes it searchable for
investigators. It breaks it down into keywords: someone can search `Red pickup near downtown
yesterday", "White van with roof rack". If multiple Flock cameras are deployed, it will reconstruct a
person or vehicle's route. So how is this, and other systems being used? We have already heard from
Flock how many cases it claims to have supported. But as expected, the AI isn't always accurate. Utah
resident Brad Johnston was charged with felony damage to an Uber driver's vehicle after facial
recognition technology identified him as a 94% match from a screenshot and linked it to his driver's
license photo. The AI result became key evidence against him, but Johnston proved he was not
involved. He had not taken the Uber ride or been at the location, and the actual suspect was later
identified. After five months, the case was dismissed.
Robert Dillon In 2023, Robert Dillon was arrested in Florida for allegedly luring a child away from a
McDonald's—despite being 300 miles away when the crime occurred. Police relied on an AI system
that identified him as a 93% match to the suspect. Dillon told officers they had the wrong person, but
he spent the night in jail and had to borrow money to post bond. The charges were eventually
dropped, and his record was cleared. Dillon said the experience still haunts him, stating that police
relied on "dangerous technology instead of doing their jobs and actually investigating."
Lindsey Isaacs In Florida, Lindsey Isaacs spent 13 days in jail falsely charged with triple homicide.
Her car was simply the same make and model. Different color, no damage and a partial plate that
didn't match.
Remember when I mentioned that nationwide APLR networks can be accessed without probable
cause or a warrant? That access creates opportunities for misuse. Across the country, there have been
numerous cases of officers using ALPR systems to monitor romantic partners, ex partners, or
individuals they had personal interests in. A Wisconsin officer tracked a romantic partner and one of
her exes nearly 18o times in two months. In Florida, a sheriff's deputy used ALPR data to track and
pull over a woman he met while working security on a film set. In Jerome County, Idaho, a sheriff
used Flock to search for his wife's vehicle more than boo times. Other cases show how these systems
can impact innocent citizens. ICE and Border Patrol have used Flock over 4,000 times in immigration
enforcement, without using warrants. Kansas police used ALPR data to investigate a man who
criticized the department. In Colorado, a woman was falsely accused of theft based on a Flock alert,
and another family was held at gunpoint after their vehicle was mistakenly matched to a stolen
motorcycle from another state, resulting in a $1.9 million settlementfor the family. Atlanta, Georgia
has experienced one of the largest expansions ofALPR technology in the country, with more than
5,00o Flock cameras now deployed across the city. However, FBI crime data has raised questions
about the effectiveness of this massive surveillance network, showing no measurable impact on the
Atlanta Police Department's ability to solve crimes. At the same time, at least 20 Georgia public
officers have been arrested for misusing surveillance databases. We could discuss these cases all
night, but the point is clear: when surveillance systems expand without strong safeguards, innocent
people can become the ones forced to prove they did nothing wrong.
Idaho Law ID Code § 49-1432 (2025) Idaho law limits ALPR use to authorized Idaho agencies and
requires data to be used only for law enforcement or traffic purposes. Access is restricted to
authorized personnel and prohibits unrelated uses. The question is whether participation in a national
surveillance network violates Idaho law by allowing Idaho citizens' data to be accessed outside the ...
state. Flock automatically connects agencies to FlockOS, providing nationwide law enforcement
access. Many ALPR searches do not require a traditional warrant and instead rely on agency policies
or authorized purposes. Concerned citizens across Idaho are speaking out at city councils, but this
issue extends far beyond our state. At least 98 cities nationwide have canceled, rejected, or
deactivated these systems, including communities in California, Texas, Washington, Oregon,
Colorado, and others. Salmon has rejected ALPR and submitted a resolution that no money be spent
on it without the council's permission since often this is done without any public input. Clearwater
County will not be renewing due to lack of crimes solved and community concerns. In Los Altos Hills,
California, officials ended their Flock contract after residents and council members questioned
whether the cameras reduced crime enough to justify the cost and raised privacy concerns. In San
4

Marcos, Texas, the council canceled Flock after reviewing research that questioned whether
surveillance technology actually improves public safety. Charlottesville, Virginia, declined to continue
its pilot program, determining that the concerns outweighed the benefits. In Roseville, California, an
investigation found that 71% of Flock -generated alerts reviewed involved incorrect license plate reads.
There is growing concern across America about the potential misuse and abuse of surveillance
technology by law enforcement. While we may trust the individuals currently serving our community,
systems should never depend solely on personal restraint. Good policies protect citizens regardless of
who holds power. Assuming these tools will never be misused risks sacrificing both public safety and
the constitutional rights they are meant to protect. We cannot live by the idea, `If you're not doing
anything wrong, you have nothing to fear."Tell that to Robert Dillon, Brad Johnston, and the many
others who were wrongly accused and forced to prove their innocence. That same logic would mean
^ that if you are not saying anything wrong, you do not need free speech. But we understand that is not
how rights work. Rights are not reserved for people under suspicion —they are protections for every
citizen, especially innocent people. Privacy is a fundamental right, not a reward for innocence. The
issue isn't whether you're doing anything wrong. The issue is whether the government should monitor
innocent people without cause, or before any crime has occurred or is suspected to occur. In America,
the government generally needs a reason before searching your home, reading your mail, or tracking
you. That's the principle behind the Fourth Amendment. The Bill of Rights wasn't written to protect
criminals. It was written to protect ordinary citizens from excessive government power. The founders
assumed government power would eventually be abused if left unchecked. We talked about mission
creep. Today's promise may be: "We're only looking for stolen cars" Tomorrow it becomes:
• Outstanding warrants
• Minor traffic violations
• Tax collection
• Protest attendance
• Political demonstrations
• Firearm owner tracking
• Routine movement history
• Religious affiliation
Technology almost always expands beyond its original purpose unless laws clearly limit it.
One month ago, I called for transparency regarding any discussions involving automated
surveillance networks, a ban on AI surveillance and mass surveillance tools, and a comprehensive
ow privacy ordinance establishing clear standards for future technologies. Today, I have provided a
draft ordinance designed to protect Rexburg citizens' rights and privacy from AT surveillance systems
and technologies that can be misused.
I ask this council to take proactive action by reviewing this ordinance, allowing public input, and
considering amendments to the City of Rexburg code that establish these protections.
Again, I appreciate the time you've provided for me to share this information with you. I much rather
be at home with my family than talking about this! But this is important to me and more than 20,000
other Idahoans engaged in Deflocking Idaho. Technology is advancing faster than the laws that
govern it. Once a surveillance network is built, history shows it almost never becomes smaller —it
expands. The question before this council isn't whether today's officials can be trusted. It's whether the
laws we adopt today will protect Rexburg residents decades from now. George Orwell said it best. A
powerful quote from the book 1984 is: `Nothing was your own except the few cubic centimeters inside
your skull." It should never come close to that.
