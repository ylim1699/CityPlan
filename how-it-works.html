The 60-second version

The Growth Atlas is a 3D map of Rexburg that shows how growth and approved projects change traffic, by hour and by year (2024 to 2034). It answers one planner question: if we approve this project here, which streets get worse, and is there a better spot or road fix?

Under the map is a small version of the four-step travel model that state DOTs and planning agencies use:

  1. Trip generation. Every resident and student in each zone makes car trips (Census commute data, national trip rates, campus rules).
  2. Trip distribution. Work trips follow real Census home-to-job pairs (LODES); other trips go to stores and jobs by a gravity rule (closer and bigger wins).
  3. Mode. The share who drive comes from the Census commute survey for each neighborhood; students in the walk district drive far less.
  4. Assignment. Each hour's trips are routed on the real street network in four batches; streets that fill up slow down (BPR curve), so later trips avoid them.

The result is cars per hour on every street, divided by what that street can carry. That ratio (V/C) colors the street. We then checked it against 89 city traffic counts: it ranks streets in roughly the right order (correlation 0.78) but misses many single streets, so it is for comparing options, not quoting exact volumes.

On top of that model sit the features: problem cards, fix comparisons, an overview dashboard, a Build mode for placing your own projects, alternate-site and road suggestions, and an AI agent that reads council and P&Z minutes to find approved projects (a person confirms each one).

How the pieces connect

Data flows left to right. Each box links to its section. The features at the end all call the same model, so anything you change upstream (a population number, a project, a fix) changes every view.

  1. Raw dataOpenStreetMap, city GIS, Census LODES and ACS, ITD counter, city minutes
  2. Street networkLanes × per-lane capacity; length ÷ speed

  4. ProjectsHousing adds people; stores add visits; road work changes capacity
  5. Zones143 places where trips start and end
  6. Car tripsWork, other, not-home-based, student
  7. DestinationsLODES pairs, gravity, campus, Walmart
  8. Outside trafficThrough traffic and visitors at 12 gateways
  9. Hour shareITD counter 059: 9.7% of the day at 5 pm

Part B

The model, step by step

Each step: what it does in plain words, the equation, the real numbers, and the file it lives in.

Step 1

Streets, capacity and travel time

scripts/build_city.py → public/data/city-rexburg.json, graph-rexburg.json · src/model/atlas.ts (CLS, SEGS)

OpenStreetMap streets are split at intersections (any coordinate shared by two or more streets). That gives 731 roads, 3,647 drawn segments, and a routing graph of 1,106 nodes and 1,426 links, 99% connected.

Capacity

Capacity is the most cars that can pass a point in one hour before traffic backs up.

C = through lanes × per-lane rate for the road typePer direction on a two-way street: half of that (the router uses C × 0.5 for each direction).
Road type (OSM tag)Cars / lane / hourDefault speedWhy
Highway (motorway: US-20)1,80065 mphNo stops; about one car every 2 seconds per lane
Ramp1,40035 mphMerging
Arterial (trunk, primary: 2nd East, Main St)90035 mphSignals give green about half the time
Minor (secondary)85035 mphSignals and more turns
Collector (tertiary)70030 mphStop signs and turning cars
Local (residential)45025 mphStop signs, parked cars, driveways

Lanes: from the OSM lanes tag when present; otherwise untagged arterials get 4 through lanes and other roads get a type default. North 2nd East is set to 4 through lanes because it was widened in 2024–25. Lanes drive everything: a wrong lane count pushes traffic onto the wrong streets, so check lanes first when a street looks wrong.

Example: 2nd East, 4 through lanes × 900 = 3,600 cars an hour both ways, 1,800 each way.

Free-flow travel time

t0 = length ÷ speedSpeed is the posted limit from OSM (86 roads have one) or the road-type default above (645 roads).

Example: half a mile at 35 mph takes 0.5 ÷ 35 × 60 = 0.86 min, about 51 seconds.

What can change capacity

  • Road work on the timeline: each project lists a factor during and after work (2nd East sewer: ×0.5 during; Main Street resurfacing: ×0.9; 2nd West / 2nd South signal: ×0.9 during, ×1.08 after), applied to its named streets within a radius.
  • Undated developer widenings (10 on the city's map): ×1.8 on main-street segments inside the widening outline, off unless a planner picks it.
  • Tested fixes: turn lanes ×1.15, signal timing ×1.08, roundabout ×1.25, on that street within about 320 m of the problem point.

Population, year by year

Step 3

Projects on the timeline

public/data/projects.json · atlas.ts projState(), projTrips() · candidates.ts PLACE_TYPES

Each project has a status (construction, approved, planned), start and end dates as decimal years, a size, a source link, a confidence level and flags for what to check.

progress = (t − start) ÷ (end − start), clamped to 0…1Before start: future. Between: under construction. After end: done. Phased projects (Madison Commons) count trips in proportion to progress; others count only when done.

How a project reaches the traffic model

  • Housing with units adds people to its zone: units × 3.3 people (or 1 per unit for senior housing). Those people then make trips like everyone else.
  • Student housing with beds adds students to its zone; they follow the student rules (fewer car trips in the walk district).
  • Everything else with trips (stores, temple, offices) adds its daily car trips as visits to and from homes, by the gravity rule, and adds attraction to its zone.
  • Road work changes capacity (step 1), not trips.

The "trips a day" shown on a housing project's card is a display figure; the routed model builds that traffic from the added people instead.

Step 4

Zones: where trips start and end

scripts/build_zones.py → public/data/zones.json

Putting people in zones

  1. Each Census block group's ACS population is spread over its zones by building floor area (footprint × floors, floors = height ÷ 3.3 m).
  2. Students vs residents comes from building type: apartments and large untagged buildings inside the walk district are student housing (an estimate; BYU-I bed counts per complex are not published).
  3. Totals are scaled so zones add up exactly to 22,100 residents and 21,900 students, then scaled again by the population model each year.
  4. Workers (12,161) and jobs (14,778, split into retail, food, education and other) are LODES counts per Census block.
  5. Each zone connects to up to 3 nearby street points within 700 m, so its trips can leave by more than one street.

Commute mode per zone

From ACS B08301 for the zone's block group, blended toward the county value when the sample is small:

value = w × block group + (1 − w) × county, w = n ÷ (n + 300)n = workers surveyed in the block group. A block group with 300 workers gets half its own value, half the county's.

County values: 81.3% of commuters who travel drive, 1.106 people per car, 13.3% work from home, 13.9% walk.

Step 5

Car trips from every zone

src/model/routing.ts · tripTable()

For each town zone i, a day of person trips, then the share by car. Every trip is a round trip: half goes out, half comes back.

Residents

W = 2 × workers × grow × (1 − wfh) work person-trips
PT = R × 3.0 all person-trips
NHB = 0.25 × PT not-home-based
HBO = PT − W − NHB home-based other

work car trips = W × drive ÷ occ
other car trips = (HBO or NHB) × 0.6 ÷ 1.5 × clamp(drivezone ÷ drivecounty, 0.6, 1.2) grow = this year's residents ÷ 2026 residents in the zone. drive, occ and wfh are the zone's ACS values. The last factor assumes neighborhoods where people walk to work also walk more for errands.

Students

student car trips = S × 3 × carShare × m ÷ 1.33 = student trips a day (tuned); carShare = 55% (students with a car); 1.3 people per car; m = 0.25 inside the walk district, 0.5 within 2.5 km of campus, 0.7 farther out. 70% go to campus (split by the campus lot shares), 30% by the gravity rule.

Worked example (before the α scale below): a walk-district zone with 1,000 students: 1,000 × 3 × 0.55 × 0.25 ÷ 1.3 = 317 car trips a day. The same students 3 km out: 1,000 × 3 × 0.55 × 0.7 ÷ 1.3 = 888. That gap is why the walk-district site wins the placement test.

Then everything is scaled

every in-town trip × α (α = 2)α is the single trip-scale factor tuned against counts. It is applied before outside traffic is worked out.

With default settings this gives about 171,000 car trips a day in fall 2026 (both legs counted), rising to about 187,000 by fall 2034 with growth alone and 201,000 with the planned projects.

Step 6

Where each trip goes

src/model/routing.ts · tripTable(), gravity()

Work trips: real Census pairs

LODES lists how many people live in each Census block and work in each other block. Work car trips from a zone are split over its job zones in proportion to those counts. Workers who live outside town enter through the gateway in their direction.

Other trips: the gravity rule

share to zone j = Aj e−β tij ÷ Σk Ak e−β tikBigger places pull more trips; farther places pull fewer. t = free-flow drive time in minutes + 1 minute to park (1.5 minutes within a zone). β = 0.05 per minute (tuned).
Aj = 4 × retail jobs + 4 × food jobs + 1 × other jobs + 1 × education jobs + 0.5 × households + project visits

Example: with β = 0.05, a store 10 minutes away keeps e−0.5 = 61% of its pull; 20 minutes away keeps 37%.

Special rules

  • Walmart: 40% of home-based other trips are shopping, and 60% of those go to the Walmart zone from every part of town (assumed).
  • Campus: 70% of student car trips go to the 4 campus zones by their lot shares.
  • Not-home-based trips (work to lunch, store to store) start where retail, food and other jobs are, and spread by gravity.
  • Project visits (WinCo, temple, offices) go to and from homes by gravity.

Step 7

Outside traffic: visitors and through trips

src/model/routing.ts · end of tripTable()

The highways carry people who don't live in town. Each gateway has a real daily count; whatever the model's own trips don't explain is outside traffic.

outsideg = max(0, countg − model's local trips at g)
through on highway gateways = outsideg × 30%
trips from a to b = througha × throughb ÷ Σ through ÷ 2 Highway gateways are those with 10,000+ a day: US-20 north, US-20 southwest, West Highway 33. The other 70% there, and all outside traffic on smaller roads, are visitors coming in to shop, study or work, spread by gravity.

Today that is about 2,500 through trips a day. Without this step the highways would look too empty.

Step 8

Hours of the day

scripts/build_hourly.py → public/data/hourly.json · routing.ts shareAt()

trips in hour h = daily trips × shareh × day factor

The shares come from ITD's permanent counter 059 on SH-33, 5.3 miles east of Main Street (November 2023 weekday average). It is a highway counter outside town, so it captures commute timing well and campus timing less well.

Step 9

Routing: trips choose streets

src/model/routing.ts · assign() · runs in src/worker/route.worker.ts

  1. Take the hour's zone-to-zone trip table.
  2. Batch 1 (40%): each zone's trips take the fastest path (Dijkstra) from all of the zone's street points, on current travel times.
  3. Update every link's time with the BPR curve using the volume loaded so far.
  4. Batches 2, 3, 4 (30%, 20%, 10%) route on the slower times, updating after each. Later trips avoid streets that filled up, the way drivers learn to avoid jams.
  5. Add each link's volume to the street segments it covers, so the 3D view can color them.

The BPR curve

t = t0 × [ 1 + 0.15 × (V ÷ C)4 ]From the federal Bureau of Public Roads. A road barely slows until it is nearly full, then slows fast.

Outputs of one run

  • Volume on every segment (both directions added), compared with total capacity for V/C.
  • Travel time index per segment: final time ÷ free-flow time.
  • Vehicle-hours traveled (VHT): Σ volume × time over all links. The difference in VHT between two scenarios is the "delay added" or "delay saved" the comparison features report.

Speed: one hour takes about 0.1 s. The worker always runs the newest request first, caches up to 300 results, and fills the hour bar, the year scan and the overview after the main run.

Noise: batch routing is not a perfect equilibrium, so two nearly identical scenarios can differ by about 1–2 vehicle-hours. The four-way project mix treats differences under 2 veh-h or 5% as noise. Treat very small "saved" numbers the same way.

Step 10

Congestion measures

The Filter's congestion buttons group these as Flowing (under 70%), Busy (70–85%) and Heavy (85%+). Problem cards flag 90% and up: near 90% one braking car or a long red light starts a backup that snowballs. These cutoffs roughly match Highway Capacity Manual level of service E (0.9–1.0) and F (over 1.0); Rexburg and ITD may use their own standard.

The count-based fallback

src/model/atlas.ts · traffic() when no routed volumes are given

The original atlas method. It needs no routing, so it paints the map instantly while the worker routes, and it is what you see if routing fails. It starts from a count and scales it.

volume = daily count × shareh × activity(place, h) ÷ day-average activity × popF + project trips nearby
popF = (R + S) ÷ (22,100 + 21,900)
activity = 0.72 + 0.55 × max over centers of e−d²/2r² × center multiplier Daily count: the street's city count grown 2.5% a year (halved on one-way streets), else a road-type default (12,000 arterial, 7,000 minor, 4,000 collector, 500 local). Centers: campus (busier mornings ×1.3), Walmart retail (evenings ×1.3), downtown. Project trips are spread over main streets within 900 m, weighted by road type and distance.

The hourly shape here is the older assumed profile (7.5% at 8 am, 9.5% at 5 pm). Once routing finishes, the routed volumes replace all of this, and the city counts become the test instead of the input.

Part C

Features: what people see, and what drives each one

Two modes share one map. Resident is a read-only view of what is changing. Planner (the default) adds the tools to test projects and fixes.

Start screen and records check

src/components/StartScreen.tsx · src/hooks/useRecords.ts · api/records.ts · agent/laserfiche.ts

The front door states the job: "See what a project does to traffic before you approve it." The city loads behind it. It shows how current the city records are (latest council minutes, P&Z minutes and P&Z agendas already read).

Check for new records lists the newest records in the city's Laserfiche archive, reads up to 6 unseen ones (newest first) with the AI agent, and puts what it finds in the planner's review list. You can enter the city while it reads. Nothing reaches the map until a person confirms it.

Being a good neighbor to the archive: the city's archive allows only a few public sessions at once. The server uses one session per check, signs out 20 seconds after its last request, and reports "the archive is at its limit" instead of retrying in a loop.

Alternate sites, AI recommendations and Suggest sites

Stage 1: screen open land (instant)

A grid of points across town (about 9,000 cells) keeps the ones that pass the placement rules. Each gets a score; lower is better:

access = km to the nearest main street (must be ≤ 1.2)
score = 3 × max(0, access − 0.4) + 4 × max(0, nearby load − 0.7) + type rule nearby load = worst V/C on main streets within 300 m right now. The best 8 sites, at least 800 m apart, go to stage 2.

Stage 2: the router judges (a few seconds)

The worker routes the selected year and hour once per site, with your other planned projects in place, and ranks by vehicle-hours added.   • Parking adds no trips, so the router can't rank it; it keeps the screen's order (walking distance to campus).

Access roads: the slime-mold search

src/model/physarum.ts · PlannerWorkspace.tsx AccessRoads

For a placed project, "Suggest access roads" proposes new connecting roads. A slime mold (Physarum) grows tubes between food sources and thickens the ones that carry flow; Tero et al. (2010, Science) showed it rebuilds a network like the Tokyo rail system. We use that rule to propose roads, then the router decides.

  1. Food sources: the nearest street node in each of 8 compass directions within 2.8 km, preferring bigger streets (score = distance ÷ (1 + 0.35 × road rank)).
  2. Cost grid (about 2,400 cells): open land 1, along existing streets 1.8, parking lot 2.5, park 4, building 14, water 60.
  3. 36 iterations: push flow from the site to one random food source; solve pressures with conjugate gradients; each tube's conductance moves toward a sigmoid of its flow: D ← D + 0.6 × (2 f(Q) − D), f(Q) = Q1.6 ÷ (1 + Q1.6). Strong tubes win, the rest wither.
  4. Read off the strongest tube to each food source, simplify it, drop any that cross a building, keep the top 5.
  5. The router judges each as a two-lane collector (700 cars/hour each way, 35 mph) and keeps those that cut total delay, ranked by saving per meter, top 3. A saving over 20 veh-h is flagged: it is probably a shortcut for through traffic.

Cost, right-of-way and grades are not checked. Moving the project clears its roads.

AI agent for city records

agent/extract.ts, gemini.ts, pdftext.ts · api/agent.ts · src/model/review.ts, candidates.ts, geocode.ts · AgentPanel.tsx

The part no competitor does: read council and P&Z minutes and find approved projects, each with a quote you can check. A person confirms every one before it counts.

  1. Input: upload minutes (PDF or text), paste a Laserfiche link, or use the start screen's records check.
  2. Text layer: the server pulls the PDF's text (the city's PDFs carry OCR) so quotes can be checked later.
  3. Extraction: Gemini reads the record with a fixed instruction and a strict JSON schema (name, type, category, status, stage, worth, where, units, beds, sq ft, acres, years, plain sentence, verbatim quote, page, confidence, flags). Fallback chain: gemini-3.5-flash-lite → 3.6-flash → 3.1-flash-lite → 4-hydrogen; any failure or malformed answer moves to the next. Claude is optional (AGENT_PROVIDER=claude).
  4. No quote, no entry: items with a quote of 20 characters or less are dropped.
  5. Quote check (by the server, not the AI): each part of the quote must appear in the record. Exact match = found word for word; a same-length window sharing 80%+ of words = close (allows OCR slips like "Term River"); otherwise NOT found: check by hand.
  6. Screen: only items that put something new at a place are reviewed (buildings, new or widened roads, rezoning a parcel). Things already built, votes that build nothing (naming, bonds, budgets), studies and utilities are set aside but can be brought back.
  7. Merge: the same project in several records becomes one card (name tokens overlap, or the same street address). The most advanced record leads; blanks are filled from the others; conflicting addresses are flagged.
  8. Map spot: addresses on Rexburg's grid ("1600 N 2nd E", "620 S 2530 W") get a suggested spot from the street grid. City streets count blocks (2nd East = 200 East); county roads count 1,000 to the mile.
  9. A person places it: checks the quote, clicks the map, picks type, size and opening year (defaults from the record), then confirms. Trips come from the rate table in step 3; anything the reviewer chose is flagged.
  10. It joins the model in this browser and is marked "Found by the AI agent, confirmed by a person".

Status so far: projects.candidates.json holds 21 items found from the minutes (Home Depot Subdivision, Moody Townhomes PUD, Summerfield Division 14, University Towne Center Plat and others). Say "our AI read the records" on stage only for projects that are confirmed.

Security: API keys stay on the server. If AGENT_TOKEN is set, callers need the passcode, so strangers can't spend the quota.

Part D

Trust: how well it matches reality

Four checks: standard method, matches counts, holds up on counts it never saw, and results make sense. It passes the first and last; the middle two are where the work is.

Accuracy against city counts

How it was tuned

  1. 20 of the 89 counts are held out by a fixed random shuffle (seed 7) and never used for tuning.
  2. 288 combinations are tried: α ∈ {1.5 … 3.5} (8 values) × β ∈ {0.05, 0.08, 0.1, 0.14} × student trips ∈ {2.5, 3, 3.5} × through share ∈ {30%, 50%, 70%}.
  3. Each is routed at 5 pm today and scored; the winner has the most tuning counts under GEH 5 (ties: lowest mean GEH).
  4. The winner is then scored on the 20 held-out counts and on 26 intersection approach counts from 2023 (two-way leg volume read as 2 × approach).

What the numbers mean

The model ranks streets in roughly the right order but misses many single streets. Busy streets come out busy and quiet ones quiet (correlation 0.78), which is what comparisons need. Exact volumes on one street are often off by a lot. Adding ACS, the 2023 counts and the ITD hourly shape did not move the score, so the limit is structural:

  • Zones are coarse (1.2 km), so trips load onto a few connector streets; streets next to a connector get too much, parallel streets get none.
  • Batch assignment is not full equilibrium.
  • Lane counts are mostly OSM defaults; a wrong lane count shifts traffic between parallel streets.
  • The counts are old (2015–2020, grown 2.5%/yr) and predate the US-20 interchange and 2nd East widening.

How to say it: "It ranks streets in roughly the right order, correlation about 0.8. About a third of counts pass the strict GEH test, and agencies aim for 85%. So use it to compare options, not to quote exact numbers."

Limits

  • Future years are what-ifs from the growth settings; projects and counts are real and sourced.
  • Weekday, fall semester only in the app. The model has Saturday, Sunday and a spring-semester factor (17,326 ÷ 24,111 students), but there is no switch for them yet.
  • Students are placed by building floor area, not by BYU-I bed counts per complex (not published).
  • No local travel survey: non-work trip rates are national averages scaled by one factor (α = 2).
  • Not an intersection or signal-timing study. No turning movements, queues or special events. Fixes are preset capacity changes.
  • Zoning, ownership, utilities and cost are not checked for placed projects or suggested roads.
  • Parking supply is very rough (lot area ÷ 30 m²).
  • Confirmed AI projects and planner ghosts live in one browser; nothing is written back to projects.json from the app.

The one-line close: "It doesn't replace an engineering study. It tells a city which options are worth one."

Part E

Answers

Questions judges, city staff and engineers are likely to ask, with answers grounded in the sections above.

Likely questions

The method

Is this a real traffic model or a visualization?

A real one, small but standard: the four-step model (trip generation, distribution, mode, assignment) with BPR slowdown, the same structure planning agencies use. It routes about 171,000 car trips a day through 1,426 street links, hour by hour. It is roughly 85–90% of a professional model's structure; the main gaps are a local travel survey and a full mode-choice model.

How is this different from Google Maps traffic?

Google shows today's measured speeds. We forecast: what happens in 2030 if a grocery store opens at a given spot, before anyone builds it. Google can't run a "what if"; we can't measure today as precisely as Google.

What's the BPR formula and why use it?

t = t₀ × (1 + 0.15 × (V/C)⁴), from the federal Bureau of Public Roads. Roads barely slow until nearly full, then slow sharply: +1% at half full, +10% at 90%, +31% at 120%. It is the default in almost every travel model. See step 9.

Why route in four batches instead of all at once?

If everyone took the empty-road fastest path, one street would get everything. Loading 40%, then 30%, 20%, 10%, and slowing streets between batches, lets later trips avoid full streets. It approximates equilibrium at a fraction of the cost (0.1 s an hour), which is what makes dragging the timeline interactive.

What is the gravity model?

Trips to a place are proportional to its size and fall off with drive time: share ∝ A × e^(−β t). With β = 0.05 per minute, a place 10 minutes away keeps 61% of its pull and one 20 minutes away keeps 37%. Size A counts retail and food jobs ×4, other and education jobs ×1, households ×0.5. See step 6.

Where do work trips go?

Real Census pairs: LODES 2023 lists how many people live in each block and work in each other block. That is 4,108 zone pairs. Out-of-town commuters enter through the gateway in their direction.

What is α and why is it 2? Doesn't doubling trips look like a fudge?

α is the one trip-scale factor every small-town model has, set by matching counts. It says national trip rates under-produce traffic on Rexburg's counted streets. Likely reasons: visitors and students' non-campus trips we don't model separately, short trips within coarse zones that we don't route, and counts that skew toward busy streets. It is applied evenly to every zone, never to single streets, so comparisons between options stay fair.

The data

Where does the data come from? Is any of it made up?

Streets and buildings from OpenStreetMap; 89 traffic counts, project and street-plan layers from Madison County / Rexburg GIS; work trips and jobs from Census LODES 2023; population and commute mode from Census ACS 2020–2024; hourly shape from ITD counter 059; projects from city records and news, each linked to its source. National averages (NHTS) fill non-work trip rates. About two dozen assumed values are listed with their reasons in Every assumed number; four of them are tuned against counts. Nothing is invented without a label.

Your counts are from 2015–2020. Isn't that too old?

Yes, it's the biggest data weakness. We grow them 2.5% a year to 2026, and 84 of 89 predate the 2024 US-20 interchange and the 2nd East widening. Newer street-total counts from the city or ITD District 6 are the single most useful thing we could get.

How do you know how many students live where?

We don't, exactly. Each block group's Census population is spread over buildings by floor area, and apartments in the walk district are treated as student housing. Totals are scaled to 21,900 students (24,111 campus-based × 90.7% living in town). BYU-I approved-housing bed counts per complex would replace this estimate.

Why 44,000 people when the Census says about 40,000?

The Census undercounts students who list a home address elsewhere. 21,900 students + 22,100 residents = 44,000 sits between the Census estimate (~40k) and the city's own figure (~45k). Both starting numbers are editable in Planner → Numbers and assumptions.

Where do the hourly patterns come from?

ITD's permanent counter 059 on SH-33, November 2023 weekday average: 9.72% of the day's traffic at 5 pm, 6.76% at 8 am. It's a highway counter outside town, so it shows commute timing well and campus timing less well.

Where does the parking number come from?

Mapped parking lot area in the walk district ÷ 30 m² per space: about 6,218 spaces in 68 lots, many of them church or business lots. Need = students × 55% with a car. It's labeled "rough" everywhere because BYU-I doesn't publish per-lot counts.

Accuracy

How do you know it's right?

We checked it against the city's 89 traffic counts. It ranks streets roughly right (correlation 0.78), but only 35% of tuning counts pass the strict GEH test, none of the 20 held-out counts do, and 12% of the 2023 counts do. Agencies aim for 85%. So it's good for comparing options, not for quoting exact volumes yet. See Accuracy.

What is GEH?

The standard calibration score: GEH = √(2(M − C)² ÷ (M + C)). It's fair to big and small roads: being off by 100 cars matters more on a quiet street. Under 5 is a good match.

0% on held-out counts. Doesn't that mean it doesn't work?

It means it doesn't predict single-street volumes well. The held-out streets still fall in the right range of the scatter, and the correlation across all counts is 0.78, so the order of busy to quiet is mostly right. For "which of these options is better," the same errors appear in both runs and largely cancel. We'd never use it to say "2nd East will carry 1,640 cars at 5 pm."

What would improve accuracy?

In order: newer street counts; checking lane counts on the worst-matching streets (Street View); smaller zones with more street connectors; true equilibrium assignment; BYU-I bed counts per complex; separate hourly shapes for work, class and shopping trips; a 2019 back-cast so we don't need the 2.5% growth guess.

Which streets are worst?

Overloaded in the model: West Main Street, North 12th West, East 1st North, North 2nd West. Too empty: University Boulevard, South Center Street, West 4th South, South 1st West. The empty ones are mostly next to zones whose trips leave by a different connector; the overloaded ones are on connector paths. Both point at zone size and lane counts.

Features

What does "vehicle-hours of delay" mean?

Time lost to slow traffic, added up over every car on the road in that hour. 47 veh-h at 5 pm today is like 2,820 drivers each losing one minute. "Delay added" for a project is the difference in total vehicle-hours between routing the hour with and without it.

How does it decide when a problem starts?

The worker routes each fall from 2026 to 2034 at the selected hour and reports the first year any part of the street reaches 90% of capacity.

How does it split growth vs projects as the cause?

Projects add = V/C with projects − V/C growth only. Growth adds = V/C now − projects' part − V/C today. Both in percentage points.

How does it pick the best site for a project?

Two stages. A fast screen scores open land by street access, how loaded nearby streets already are, and where the type belongs (student housing near campus, homes and stores not right next to it). The best 8 are routed, each with the project in place, and ranked by delay added. It also reports the biggest single-street jump, because a lower total can still overload one street. Zoning and ownership are not checked. See Alternate sites.

Is the "AI recommendation" actually AI?

The site recommendation is a search plus a traffic simulation, not a language model: screen open land, then route each candidate and rank by delay. The language-model AI is the records agent that reads minutes. Be precise about which is which.

What's the slime-mold thing?

A network-growing rule from real biology: Physarum slime mold thickens tubes that carry flow and lets others wither; Tero et al. (Science, 2010) showed it rebuilds an efficient network like Tokyo's rail lines. We use it only to propose access roads that bend around buildings and water. The router then keeps only roads that actually reduce delay.

Why do the fix savings look so small?

They're measured across the whole network for one hour, and traffic re-routes either way, so a 15% capacity bump on one street often saves a few vehicle-hours. Differences under about 2 veh-h are routing noise. Small savings mean the fix barely helps, which is itself a useful answer.

The AI agent

Did the AI really read the city records?

Yes: the agent has read P&Z and council minutes and found 21 items (Home Depot Subdivision, Moody Townhomes PUD, Summerfield Division 14 and others), each with a quote. Only say "found by our AI" for projects a person has confirmed on the map, marked "Found by the AI agent".

How do you stop the AI from making things up?

Four guards. Every item must carry a verbatim quote, or it's dropped. The server (not the AI) checks the quote against the record's own text: word for word, close (80%+ of words, for OCR errors), or not found. Items about things already built or votes that build nothing are screened out. And nothing reaches the model until a person reads the quote and places it on the map.

Which AI model?

Google Gemini by default with a four-model fallback chain (3.5 Flash-Lite, 3.6 Flash, 3.1 Flash-Lite, 4 Hydrogen), because the free tier can rate-limit. Claude can be swapped in with one setting. Both use the same output schema.

Does it watch the city website automatically?

No. It reads what you upload or link, or what the start screen's "Check for new records" finds when someone clicks it (up to 6 new records per check). There is no background watcher.

Big picture

Can this work for other cities?

The data is national: OpenStreetMap, LODES and ACS cover every U.S. city, and the agent's instructions name Rexburg only in one line. A new city needs its map export, its counts, the build scripts rerun, and a new archive reader if it wants the automatic records check (the Laserfiche folders are Rexburg's). College towns get the student modules; others switch them off.

Who would use it, and what does it replace?

City planners and P&Z commissioners screening projects before approval, and residents seeing what's coming. It doesn't replace a traffic impact study; it tells a city which of many options are worth one.

What about PTV, Aimsun, StreetLight, Replica, ArcGIS Urban?

They are stronger models or richer data, but cost money and staff time a 30,000-person city rarely has. What's new here: AI that reads city records to find approved projects, and a fast what-if from free public data. A partnership pitch fits: "We turn city records into growth forecasts you can plug into your models."

No question matches. Try a shorter word.
